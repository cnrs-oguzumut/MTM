\section{The Puzzle: Why Does \code{NodeValence} Show Fake Dislocations in a Perfect Square Lattice?}
% ============================================================================

In ParaView, the simplest way to look for crystal defects in a 2D triangulated mesh is to count
how many triangles touch each atom. In our code, this number is called \code{NodeValence} (or
\code{ReferenceCoordination}).

\subsection{Why Triangle Counting Works on a Triangular Lattice}
In a 2D \textbf{triangular (hexagonal) lattice}, every atom is surrounded by $6$ nearest neighbors at
the exact same distance $a_0$. All triangles in the mesh are equilateral triangles with side lengths
$(a_0, a_0, a_0)$. Therefore:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item Every atom in a perfect triangular crystal is touched by \textbf{6 triangles}.
  \item If an atom is touched by \textbf{5} or \textbf{7} triangles, there is genuinely an extra or
        missing half-plane of atoms (a true dislocation core).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Why Triangle Counting Fails on a Square Lattice}
Now consider a 2D \textbf{square lattice} with grid spacing $a_0 = 1$. Each atom has only \textbf{4
nearest neighbors} (up, down, left, right at distance $1$). To build a triangular finite-element
mesh, every $1 \times 1$ square cell must be cut in half by a \textbf{diagonal edge} of length
$\sqrt{2} \approx 1.414$.

Here is the catch: every square has \textbf{two equally valid diagonals} of the exact same length
$\sqrt{2}$:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item the \textbf{forward slash} diagonal (\code{/}), or
  \item the \textbf{backslash} diagonal (\code{\textbackslash}).
\end{itemize}
Because both diagonals have the exact same length $\sqrt{2}$, tiny floating-point rounding ($10^{-15}$)
during Delaunay triangulation or remeshing will flip some squares from \code{/} to \code{\textbackslash}
\textbf{even when every single atom sits at its exact perfect square lattice position!}

Look at Figure~\ref{fig:diagonal_flip} to see what happens to the number of triangles touching each
atom when just \textbf{one single square} in a perfect grid has a flipped diagonal:

In general, any interior atom $i$ in a square lattice is surrounded by $4$ squares. It always touches
the $4$ horizontal/vertical grid edges, plus anywhere from $d_i \in \{0, 1, 2, 3, 4\}$ diagonals:
\begin{equation}
  \text{Triangle Valence at Atom } i \;=\; 4 + d_i \;\in\; \{4, 5, 6, 7, 8\}.
\end{equation}
Whenever a simulation creates regions with \code{/} diagonals next to regions with \code{\textbackslash}
diagonals, the boundary between those regions lights up with fake $5$s and $7$s!

\begin{keyidea}[Why We Need a Geometry-Aware Method]
Counting triangles only looks at \textbf{graph connectivity} (how many mesh lines meet at a point)
and ignores \textbf{where the atoms actually are in space}. To know if a dislocation is truly present,
we must measure the \textbf{Burgers vector} around a closed loop of atoms.
\end{keyidea}

\section{First Clarifying \texorpdfstring{$\bm{F} = \bm{F}_e \bm{F}_p$}{F = Fe Fp}: Elastic vs.\ Plastic Distortion}
% ============================================================================

Before introducing Method 2, let us clarify the fundamental continuum formula
\begin{equation}
  \bm{F} = \bm{F}_e \bm{F}_p
\end{equation}
and why remeshing changes how we use it.

Suppose we take a single square cell of atoms and shear the top row to the right by \textbf{one full
lattice spacing} $a_0$ (Figure~\ref{fig:FeFp}):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Total deformation gradient $\bm{F}$:} Compared to who each atom was originally connected to at $t=0$, the cell has been sheared by $\gamma = 1$:
        \[
          \bm{F} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.
        \]
  \item \textbf{Plastic slip $\bm{F}_p$:} Because shifting a crystal by exactly $1$ full lattice spacing brings the atoms right back onto the ideal square grid, this shear costs \textbf{zero energy}. It is a pure lattice-invariant plastic slip:
        \[
          \bm{F}_p = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \in \mathrm{SL}(2,\Z), \qquad \bm{M} = \bm{F}_p^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix},
        \]
        where $\bm{M}$ is the integer matrix found by \textbf{Lagrange reduction} in our code!
  \item \textbf{Elastic distortion $\bm{F}_e$:} Once we strip away the integer plastic slip $\bm{F}_p$, what remains is the true elastic stretch/rotation of the crystal bonds:
        \[
          \bm{F}_e = \bm{F} \bm{F}_p^{-1} = \bm{F} \bm{M} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \bm{I}.
        \]
        Because $\bm{F}_e = \bm{I}$, the crystal is stress-free!
\end{enumerate}

Now look at Panel (c) of Figure~\ref{fig:FeFp}: \textbf{when remeshing is turned ON}, the code
reconnects atom $B$ directly to atom $D$ (which is now right above $B$) and resets the reference
triangle! So on the new mesh, $\bm{F}_{\mathrm{new}} = \bm{I}$ and $\bm{M}_{\mathrm{new}} = \bm{I}$.
That is why we cannot simply look for jumps in $\bm{M}$ when remeshing is enabled---remeshing has
already absorbed $\bm{M}$ into the new mesh connections!

Instead, we can measure the elastic lattice step \textbf{directly from the bond vectors of the
current mesh}. That is \textbf{Method 2}.

\section{Method 2 Explained Step-by-Step: The Discrete Burgers Circuit}
% ============================================================================

In textbook physics, how do you find out if a region of crystal contains a dislocation?
You walk around a closed loop of atoms (a \textbf{Burgers circuit}), counting how many ideal
lattice steps (\emph{right, up, left, down}) you take from atom to atom.
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item If you end up back at $(0,0)$ in the ideal grid, \textbf{there is no dislocation inside the loop}.
  \item If your step count fails to close by $\bm{B} \neq (0,0)$, \textbf{there is a dislocation with Burgers vector $\bm{B}$ inside the loop!}
\end{itemize}

Method 2 applies this exact textbook idea to the \textbf{smallest possible closed loop in our mesh:
every single triangle $T$!}

\subsection{Step 1: Measure Each Directed Triangle Edge in Lattice Units}
Take any triangle $T = (A, B, C)$ oriented counter-clockwise. It has three directed edges:
$A \to B$, $B \to C$, and $C \to A$.
For an edge from atom $a$ at position $\bm{x}_a$ to atom $b$ at position $\bm{x}_b$:
\begin{equation}
  \text{Continuous bond vector in lattice units:}\qquad
  \bm{s}_{ab} = \bm{A}_0^{-1}(\bm{x}_b - \bm{x}_a),
\end{equation}
where $\bm{A}_0$ is the ideal lattice basis matrix:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item For an unrotated square lattice with spacing $a_0 = 1$, $\bm{A}_0 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, so $\bm{s}_{ab} = \bm{x}_b - \bm{x}_a$ is simply $(\Delta x, \Delta y)$ in units of $a_0$!
  \item For a triangular lattice, $\bm{A}_0 = a_0 \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1/2 \\ 0 & \sqrt{3}/2 \end{pmatrix}$ converts $(\Delta x, \Delta y)$ into steps along the $0^\circ$ and $60^\circ$ crystal axes.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Step 2: Round Each Bond Vector to the Nearest Ideal Integer Step \texorpdfstring{$\Delta\bm{n}_{ab}$}{Delta n\_ab}}
Even if the crystal is elastically stretched or sheared by $10\%$ or $20\%$, a bond that is supposed
to be $(1, 0)$ might have $\bm{s}_{ab} = (1.08, -0.05)$. When we round each component to the nearest
integer, we recover the \textbf{ideal integer lattice step}:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta\bm{n}_{ab} = \operatorname{round}(\bm{s}_{ab}) =
  \begin{pmatrix} \operatorname{round}(s_{ab,x}) \\[2pt] \operatorname{round}(s_{ab,y}) \end{pmatrix} \in \Z^2.
  \label{eq:round_step}
\end{equation}
For example, $\operatorname{round}(1.08, -0.05) = (1, 0)$.

\subsection{Step 3: Sum the Three Integer Steps Around the Triangle}
Now walk counter-clockwise around the three edges of triangle $T = (A \to B \to C \to A)$ and add
the three integer step vectors together:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;\bm{B}_T \;=\; \Delta\bm{n}_{AB} + \Delta\bm{n}_{BC} + \Delta\bm{n}_{CA} \;\in\; \Z^2.\;}
  \label{eq:BT_def}
\end{equation}
This integer vector $\bm{B}_T = (B_{T,x}, B_{T,y})$ is the \textbf{Discrete Burgers Vector of triangle $T$}.

\subsection{Worked Example 1: Why \code{/} and \code{\textbackslash} Diagonals ALWAYS Give \texorpdfstring{$\bm{B}_T = (0,0)$}{B\_T = (0,0)}}

Let us test Eq.~\eqref{eq:BT_def} on a square lattice with \code{/} vs.\ \code{\textbackslash}
diagonals (Figure~\ref{fig:worked_perfect}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Case (a) --- Triangle with a \code{/} diagonal:}
        Vertices at $A=(0,0)$, $B=(1,0)$, $C=(1,1)$.
        \begin{align*}
          A \to B: &\quad \Delta\bm{n}_{AB} = (1,0) - (0,0) = (+1,\; 0) \\
          B \to C: &\quad \Delta\bm{n}_{BC} = (1,1) - (1,0) = (\;0, +1) \\
          C \to A: &\quad \Delta\bm{n}_{CA} = (0,0) - (1,1) = (-1, -1) \quad\text{(the \code{/} diagonal!)}
        \end{align*}
        Add them up:
        \[
          \bm{B}_T = (+1, 0) + (0, +1) + (-1, -1) = \bm{(0, 0)}.
        \]
  \item \textbf{Case (b) --- Triangle with a flipped \code{\textbackslash} diagonal:}
        Vertices at $A=(0,0)$, $B=(1,0)$, $D=(0,1)$.
        \begin{align*}
          A \to B: &\quad \Delta\bm{n}_{AB} = (1,0) - (0,0) = (+1,\; 0) \\
          B \to D: &\quad \Delta\bm{n}_{BD} = (0,1) - (1,0) = (-1, +1) \quad\text{(the \code{\textbackslash} diagonal!)} \\
          D \to A: &\quad \Delta\bm{n}_{DA} = (0,0) - (0,1) = (\;0, -1)
        \end{align*}
        Add them up:
        \[
          \bm{B}_T = (+1, 0) + (-1, +1) + (0, -1) = \bm{(0, 0)}.
        \]
\end{itemize}

Look at Figure~\ref{fig:worked_perfect}: because Method 2 knows that a diagonal edge has integer step
$(\pm 1, \pm 1)$ instead of treating it like a $(1,0)$ nearest-neighbor bond, \textbf{every triangle
in a perfect or elastically deformed square lattice has $\bm{B}_T = (0,0)$, regardless of how its
diagonals are connected!}

\subsection{Worked Example 2: What Happens at a Real Dislocation Core?}

Now let us see what happens when there \textbf{is} a real edge dislocation inside the crystal!
Suppose the bottom row has an extra atom squeezed in (an extra vertical half-plane of atoms, i.e.\
an edge dislocation with Burgers vector $\bm{B} = (+1, 0)$).

Look at the core triangle $T^* = (A \to B \to C \to A)$ right at the tip of the extra half-plane in
Figure~\ref{fig:worked_core}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Along the bottom edge $A \to B$: atom $B$ is one grid spacing to the right of $A$ (slightly compressed to $\Delta x = 0.85$), which rounds to
        \[
          \Delta\bm{n}_{AB} = \operatorname{round}(0.85,\; 0.0) = (+1,\; 0).
        \]
  \item Along the right edge $B \to C$: atom $C$ on the upper row sits at $\Delta x = -0.42$ to the left of $B$ and $\Delta y = +1.0$ up. Because $|-0.42| < 0.5$, it rounds to a pure vertical step:
        \[
          \Delta\bm{n}_{BC} = \operatorname{round}(-0.42,\; +1.0) = (0,\; +1).
        \]
  \item Along the left edge $C \to A$: going from $C$ back down to $A$ has $\Delta x = -0.43$ and $\Delta y = -1.0$. Because $|-0.43| < 0.5$, it \emph{also} rounds to a pure vertical step:
        \[
          \Delta\bm{n}_{CA} = \operatorname{round}(-0.43,\; -1.0) = (0,\; -1).
        \]
\end{enumerate}
Now add the three integer steps around the core triangle $T^*$:
\begin{equation}
  \bm{B}_{T^*} = \Delta\bm{n}_{AB} + \Delta\bm{n}_{BC} + \Delta\bm{n}_{CA}
  = (+1, 0) + (0, +1) + (0, -1) = \bm{(+1, 0)} \neq (0,0)!
\end{equation}
Bingo! The core triangle $T^*$ immediately lights up with $\bm{B}_{T^*} = (+1, 0)$, pinpointing the
exact location and the exact Burgers vector of the dislocation!

\section{How Bigger Loops Work Automatically: Cancellation of Interior Edges}
% ============================================================================

What if we want the Burgers circuit around a \textbf{whole group of triangles}---for example, the
\textbf{1-ring of triangles surrounding an atom $i$}, or a region containing a \textbf{dislocation
junction}?

Here is the most beautiful property of Method 2: \textbf{you never have to trace outer loops by hand!
You just add $\bm{B}_T$ of the triangles inside the region.}

Why? Look at Figure~\ref{fig:stokes}(a), where two adjacent triangles $T_1$ and $T_2$ share an
interior edge between atoms $A$ and $B$:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item When we walk counter-clockwise around $T_1$, we cross the shared edge from $A \to B$, adding $+\Delta\bm{n}_{AB}$.
  \item When we walk counter-clockwise around $T_2$, we cross the \textbf{exact same edge in the opposite direction} $B \to A$, adding $\Delta\bm{n}_{BA} = -\Delta\bm{n}_{AB}$.
\end{itemize}
When we add $\bm{B}_{T_1} + \bm{B}_{T_2}$, the shared interior edge $+\Delta\bm{n}_{AB} - \Delta\bm{n}_{AB} = (0,0)$
\textbf{cancels out in exact integer arithmetic!}

This gives us three powerful results immediately:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Unit-Cell Square Plaquette Filter (Figure~\ref{fig:stokes}a):}
        In a square lattice, the diagonal $A \leftrightarrow B$ has length $\sqrt{2}\,a_0 \approx 1.414\,a_0$, which is longer than the four outer sides of the square ($1.0\,a_0$). If strong elastic shear causes \emph{only} that internal diagonal to round by $\pm 1$ while the four outer edges of the square are still perfect, then $T_1$ gets $+\bm{B}$ and $T_2$ gets $-\bm{B}$, so their sum on the 4-atom square is $\bm{B}_{T_1} + \bm{B}_{T_2} = (0,0)$. The code automatically detects when two triangles sharing a diagonal edge ($L > 1.15\,a_0$) have $\bm{B}_{T_1} + \bm{B}_{T_2} = (0,0)$ and cancels that pair to zero!
  \item \textbf{Nodal 1-Ring Burgers Vector (Figure~\ref{fig:stokes}b):}
        For every atom $i$, summing $\bm{B}_T$ over all triangles touching atom $i$,
        \begin{equation}
          \bm{B}_i^{(1\text{-ring})} = \sum_{T \ni i} \bm{B}_T,
        \end{equation}
        gives the exact Burgers circuit around the 1-ring polygon of neighbors of atom $i$!
        \begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
          \item If $\bm{B}_i^{(1\text{-ring})} = (0,0)$, atom $i$ is in a defect-free crystal environment, so we set \code{ReferenceCoordination} $= 6$.
          \item If $\bm{B}_i^{(1\text{-ring})} \neq (0,0)$, atom $i$ sits directly on a true dislocation core, and we preserve its $5/7$ defect label!
        \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Dislocation Junctions (Frank's Rule, Figure~\ref{fig:junction}):}
        What if a horizontal edge dislocation $\bm{B}_1 = (+1, 0)$ (gliding in from the left boundary in \code{Shift / Staircase}) collides with a vertical edge dislocation $\bm{B}_2 = (0, +1)$ (gliding up from the bottom boundary) to form a \textbf{dislocation junction}?
        By Discrete Stokes' Theorem, any circuit enclosing the junction automatically sums the Burgers vectors of the reacting dislocations:
        \begin{equation}
          \bm{B}_{\mathrm{junction}} = \sum_{T \subset \Omega_{\mathrm{junction}}} \bm{B}_T = \bm{B}_1 + \bm{B}_2 = (+1, 0) + (0, +1) = (+1, +1),
        \end{equation}
        with magnitude $\|\bm{b}_{\mathrm{junction}}\|_2 = \sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.414\,a_0$!
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Summary: Does Method 2 Look Only at a Single Triangle?}

\textbf{No!} Method 2 combines \textbf{three nested loop sizes} that work together via Discrete Stokes' Theorem~\cite{ArizaOrtiz2005,Stukowski2010,Stukowski2012}:

  \centering
  \small
  \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{1.6cm}p{2.9cm}p{3.8cm}p{6.5cm}@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Level} & \textbf{Circuit Size} & \textbf{Formula} & \textbf{Physical Role in the Code} \\
    \midrule
    \textbf{Level 1} & \textbf{1 Triangle}\newline (3 atoms) & $\bm{B}_T = \Delta\bm{n}_{01} + \Delta\bm{n}_{12} + \Delta\bm{n}_{20}$ & Elementary building block (\code{ElementBurgersMagnitude}). Because any large loop is tiled by triangles and interior edges cancel, the non-zero Burgers vector of a dislocation is concentrated in the core triangle $T^*$ where slip crosses $0.5\,a_0$. \\
    \textbf{Level 2} & \textbf{Square Unit Cell}\newline (4 atoms, $T_1 \cup T_2$) & $\bm{B}_{\mathrm{square}} = \bm{B}_{T_1} + \bm{B}_{T_2}$ & \textbf{Anti-noise filter:} If elastic shear causes only a shared square diagonal ($\sqrt{2}\,a_0$) to round by $\pm 1$, then $\bm{B}_{T_1} = -\bm{B}_{T_2}$ and the 4-atom square loop has $\bm{B}_{\mathrm{square}} = (0,0)$, so both cancel to zero. \\
    \textbf{Level 3} & \textbf{Nodal 1-Ring}\newline (5--7 triangles around atom $i$) & $\bm{B}_i^{(1\text{-ring})} = \sum_{T \ni i} \bm{B}_T$ & \textbf{Full polygon Burgers circuit around atom $i$} (\code{BurgersMagnitude} \& \code{ReferenceCoordination}). All spoke edges touching atom $i$ cancel out, leaving the closed loop of neighbor atoms around $i$. \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\section{Historical Background and Literature Lineage}
% ============================================================================

Method 2 rests on four foundational developments in dislocation theory and computational mechanics:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The Classical Discrete Burgers Circuit --- Burgers (1939) \& Frank (1951):}
        J.~M.~Burgers~\cite{Burgers1939} introduced the Burgers vector $\bm{b}$ to characterize a
        dislocation line via the closure failure of an encircling loop. F.~C.~Frank~\cite{Frank1951}
        formulated the discrete atom-to-atom \emph{Finish-to-Start / Right-Hand} (FS/RH) Burgers
        circuit by mapping each interatomic bond in a defective crystal onto its corresponding
        integer step $\Delta\bm{n}_{ab}$ in an ideal reference Bravais lattice, and proved Frank's
        conservation law ($\bm{B}_{\mathrm{junction}} = \bm{B}_1 + \bm{B}_2$) at dislocation nodes
        and junctions.
  \item \textbf{The Continuum Elastic Pull-Back ($\bm{F}_e^{-1}$) and Nye's Tensor --- Nye (1953), Bilby et al.\ (1955) \& Kr\"oner (1960):}
        J.~F.~Nye~\cite{Nye1953}, B.~A.~Bilby, R.~Bullough, and E.~Smith~\cite{Bilby1955}, and
        E.~Kr\"oner~\cite{Kroner1960} established the differential-geometric continuum theory of
        dislocations: decomposing the total distortion into elastic and plastic parts
        ($\bm{F} = \bm{F}_e \bm{F}_p$, generalized to $\mathrm{GL}(d,\Z)$ lattice-invariant shears in
        the Mesoscopic Tensorial Model by Conti and Zanzotto~\cite{ContiZanzotto2004}) and defining
        the continuum Burgers vector via the circulation of the inverse elastic distortion,
        $\bm{b}(\mathcal{C}) = -\oint_{\mathcal{C}} \bm{F}_e^{-1}\,d\bm{y} = -\int_{\Omega}\operatorname{Curl}(\bm{F}_e^{-1})\,dA$.
        C.~S.~Hartley and Y.~Mishin~\cite{HartleyMishin2005} later bridged the continuum Nye tensor
        with discrete atomistic neighbor bonds.
  \item \textbf{Discrete Exterior Calculus on Crystal Triangulations --- Ariza \& Ortiz (2005):}
        M.~P.~Ariza and M.~Ortiz~\cite{ArizaOrtiz2005} formulated discrete crystal elasticity and
        defects on triangulated Bravais graphs using algebraic topology (discrete exterior calculus):
        representing integer lattice bond steps as $1$-cochains $\Delta\bm{n}_{ab} \in \Z^d$ on
        directed mesh edges and defining elementary triangle Burgers vectors as the discrete exterior
        derivative ($2$-cochain coboundary) $\bm{B}_T = (d\Delta\bm{n})_T = \Delta\bm{n}_{01} + \Delta\bm{n}_{12} + \Delta\bm{n}_{20}$,
        so that Discrete Stokes' Theorem $\bm{B}(\partial\Omega) = \sum_{T \subset \Omega} \bm{B}_T$
        holds identically.
  \item \textbf{The Delaunay-Triangle Dislocation Extraction Algorithm (DXA) --- Stukowski \& Albe (2010--2012):}
        A.~Stukowski and K.~Albe~\cite{Stukowski2010} and A.~Stukowski, V.~V.~Bulatov, and
        A.~Arsenlis~\cite{Stukowski2012} (implemented in \code{OVITO}) turned the Delaunay-triangle
        Burgers circuit into a general automated algorithm for arbitrary deformed atomistic systems:
        tiling the crystal with a Delaunay tessellation, projecting each directed Delaunay edge onto
        its closest ideal Bravais lattice vector in a canonical anti-symmetric order
        ($\Delta\bm{n}_{ba} = -\Delta\bm{n}_{ab}$), and marching across elementary triangles with
        $\bm{B}_T \neq \bm{0}$. In our MTM implementation, we combine this Delaunay-triangle Burgers
        circuit with the \textbf{Level~2 square-plaquette diagonal filter} ($\bm{B}_{T_1} + \bm{B}_{T_2} = \bm{0}$
        across shared $\sqrt{2}\,a_0$ diagonals) and \textbf{Level~3 $1$-ring nodal summation} to
        handle four-fold 2D square lattices without false positives.
\end{enumerate}
